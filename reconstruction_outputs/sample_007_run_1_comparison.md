Thus, the advantage of such generalizations lies precisely in their grounding in the observable world. They may lack the elegance of exact equations, but they possess a kind of epistemic solidity that the more precise laws cannot claim.

It is therefore with such generalizations that science actually begins. "A is usually followed by B" is the nearest we can come, in practice, to a causal law. It may be that A is always followed by B under certain conditions, but we cannot know this with certainty. We cannot foresee every possible interfering factor, nor rule out the emergence of one in the future. Yet if, after a vast number of observations, we find that A is consistently followed by B, and deviations are either absent or clearly traceable to identifiable disturbances, then we are justified, for practical purposes, in saying "A causes B"—provided we abandon the metaphysical baggage traditionally attached to the word "cause." We need not suppose that there is some necessary connection, some hidden force binding A to B; it is enough that the sequence is, in fact, nearly invariable.

But there is another point, equally important, that must be faced: the assumption that every event has a single cause is almost certainly mistaken. In ordinary thought, we tend to single out one antecedent and call it *the* cause, treating the rest as mere background conditions. This habit, I suggest, has its roots not in science, but in the psychology of human action. We think of causes as analogous to acts of will: deliberate, singular, initiating. But will itself is not the simple, self-originating power it is often taken to be; and in any case, there is no reason to suppose that the physical world operates on such a model. In nature, we do not find unique causes. What we find are multiple antecedents, each of which is nearly invariable in its association with a given outcome. To pick one and elevate it above the others is arbitrary—unless we can identify an antecedent that is *always* followed by the effect, without exception. But no such antecedent is known to us. What we know are not invariable sequences, but nearly invariable ones.

Consider the hooter that sounds at noon, and the workers who immediately leave the factory for dinner. We may say, loosely, that the hooter causes their departure. But so do countless other hooters, in other factories, under similar conditions. Each is followed by the same effect. Each has the same statistical reliability. Each, therefore, has an equal claim to be called a cause. The event—the exodus of workers—has not one cause, but many. And this is typical. The world is not arranged so that each occurrence has a solitary origin; rather, it is woven from a network of antecedents, any one of which, in ordinary language, might be singled out as *the* cause. But science, if it is to be free from anthropomorphic illusion, must recognize that causation, as we can know it, is not singular, not necessary, not metaphysically grounded—but a matter of frequent conjunction, observed, tested, and confirmed within the limits of human experience.

Science, then, begins with the modest proposition: "A is, in the main, followed by B." That is the nearest surviving kin of the old causal edict.
