Rather than beginning with doubt or with first principles, Kant starts from a simple, stubborn fact: we do have knowledge—of mathematics, of nature, of moral obligation. Taking that as given, he asks: how is such knowledge possible at all? From his answer, he proceeds to derive far‑reaching metaphysical claims about the structure of reality.

Whether those final metaphysical claims stand up is very much open to debate. But even critics generally concede Kant two enormous achievements. First, he saw that we possess a priori knowledge—knowledge independent of experience—that is not merely “analytic,” not just the sort of thing whose denial would be self‑contradictory. Second, he put the theory of knowledge—the question “how do we know?”—at the very center of philosophy, rather than treating it as a side issue.

To see what was so new here, we need to linger over that word “analytic.” Before Kant, the prevailing view was that all a priori truths had to be analytic. The best way to grasp this is through deliberately boring examples. If I say, “A bald man is a man,” or “A plane figure is a figure,” or “A bad poet is a poet,” I am not exactly shaking the foundations of anyone’s worldview. In each case, the subject already contains at least two features—bald and man, plane and figure, bad and poet—and I simply restate one of those features as the predicate. These are analytic judgments: by merely analyzing the concept in the subject, you can extract the predicate. No fresh content is added.

Such propositions are almost never uttered in real life except as warm‑up acts for trickery or pedantry. Precisely because they are so trivial, they were taken as paradigms of a priori certainty. The pre‑Kantian view ran like this: whenever we know something a priori, the predicate is already, in some sense, contained in the subject. Denying such a judgment would thus be like saying “A bald man is not bald”: you would be both affirming and denying the same property of the same subject, which is blatantly self‑contradictory.

On that picture, the law of non‑contradiction—nothing can both possess and lack the same property at the same time—does all the heavy lifting. It alone guarantees the truth of everything we can know a priori. As long as you avoid contradiction, you are safely within the realm of necessary truth.

David Hume (1711–1776), who came just before Kant and accepted the standard account of what makes knowledge a priori, blew a hole in this complacent picture. He noticed that many statements traditionally treated as analytic—most famously those involving cause and effect—do not, in fact, work that way. Rationalist philosophers had typically claimed that, with enough information, you could in principle deduce the effect from the cause as a matter of sheer logic. Strike the match in the right conditions, and fire follows “by necessity.”

Hume argued—almost everyone now agrees rightly—that this is an illusion. No matter how closely you analyze the concept of the cause, you will never logically extract the effect from it. The link between them is not analytic but what he called “synthetic”: it connects distinct ideas instead of unpacking one idea into its parts. From this discovery, Hume leapt to a far more unsettling claim: we can know nothing a priori about the connection between cause and effect at all. Our expectation that the future will resemble the past is a habit of mind, not a rational insight into necessity.

For Kant, raised and trained within the rationalist tradition, Hume’s skepticism was a shock to the system. Kant later said that Hume “awoke me from my dogmatic slumber.” He set himself the task of answering Hume—not by denying Hume’s critique of analytic reasoning, but by rethinking what a priori knowledge could be.

Kant recognized that Hume’s point was not confined to causality. The same structure appears in arithmetic and geometry. Propositions of pure mathematics, he argued, are also “synthetic”: no merely logical analysis of the subject will yield the predicate. His favorite example was the apparently innocent statement: 7 + 5 = 12.

If you stick strictly to the concepts “7” and “5,” and even to the idea of “adding” them, you do not yet have the concept “12.” You must perform an actual synthesis—combine 7 and 5—to arrive at 12. The result is not contained, ready‑made, in the original concepts; it is something new that arises from bringing them together in a particular way. So while 7 + 5 = 12 is known a priori—no one consults experience to verify it—it is not analytic. It is a synthetic a priori truth.

From this insight, Kant drew a bold conclusion: all pure mathematics, and not just stray examples, consists of synthetic a priori judgments. And that conclusion opened an entirely new problem, one that would occupy the rest of his critical philosophy: if mathematics (and likewise fundamental principles like causality) are synthetic yet knowable a priori, how is such knowledge possible? His subsequent work is, in large measure, an attempt to answer that question.
